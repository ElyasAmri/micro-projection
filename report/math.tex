\section{Setup}

The projector illuminates a flat surface at (approximately) normal incidence,
aimed at a center point $O$. A telecentric-lens camera is also aimed at $O$,
with its optical axis tilted by an angle $\theta$ away from the surface
normal (Figure~\ref{fig:setup}). Because the camera's lens is telecentric,
its imaging cone is a bundle of parallel rays with a fixed rectangular
cross-section $W_0 \times H_0$ --- unlike a normal lens, this cross-section
does not change with working distance. Tilting the camera changes how that
fixed cross-section intersects the (untilted) surface plane.

Two questions follow from this geometry:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At what working distance $D$ does the projector produce an image
        whose \emph{height} equals the camera's fixed capturable height
        $H_0$?
  \item At what tilt angle $\theta$ does the camera's covered
        \emph{width} expand to match the projector's image width at that
        distance?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Camera's fixed field of view}
\label{sec:camera-fov}

A telecentric lens holds magnification $M$ constant across its focus
range, so the object-side field of view is simply the sensor size divided
by $M$:
\begin{equation}
  W_0 = \frac{w_{\text{sensor}}}{M}, \qquad
  H_0 = \frac{h_{\text{sensor}}}{M}.
\end{equation}
With $w_{\text{sensor}} = 6.14$\,mm, $h_{\text{sensor}} = 4.92$\,mm,
$M = 0.09$:
\begin{equation}
  W_0 = \frac{6.14}{0.09} = 68.22\text{\,mm}, \qquad
  H_0 = \frac{4.92}{0.09} = 54.67\text{\,mm}.
\end{equation}
This footprint is fixed anywhere in the lens's $132$--$182$\,mm focus
range; where exactly the camera sits within that range is a free choice
that does not affect $W_0, H_0$.

\section{Projector image size vs.\ working distance}

For a fixed projection lens, image size scales linearly with throw
distance (similar triangles about the lens's exit pupil). Using the
184\,mm lens's rated point $(D_0, H_{\text{proj},0}) = (184, 82)$\,mm as the
calibration point:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{proj}}(D) = D \cdot \frac{82}{184} = D \cdot \frac{41}{92},
  \qquad
  W_{\text{proj}}(D) = D \cdot \frac{131.2}{184} = D \cdot \frac{65.6}{92}.
\end{equation}
Both preserve the DMD's fixed image aspect ratio
$131.2/82 = 1.6$.

\section{Matching distance \texorpdfstring{$D$}{D}}

Set $H_{\text{proj}}(D) = H_0$:
\begin{equation}
  D = H_0 \cdot \frac{92}{41} = 54.67 \times 2.244 \approx 122.7\text{\,mm}.
\end{equation}
This lands inside the 92--184\,mm lens family's span (below the 184\,mm
lens's nominal point, above the 92\,mm lens's), so it is a modest refocus,
not an extrapolation. At this distance the projector's image is
\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{proj}}(D) = H_0 \times 1.6 = 54.67 \times 1.6 \approx 87.47\text{\,mm}
\end{equation}
wide, by $H_0 = 54.67$\,mm tall --- matching the camera's height exactly.
The projected pixel there is $50\,\mu\text{m}\times 122.7/92 \approx 67\,\mu$m.

\section{Fallback: native distance with camera crop}
\label{sec:crop}

If the 184\,mm lens cannot be refocused to $122.7$\,mm, an alternative is to
run it at its \emph{nominal} distance $D_0 = 184$\,mm and let the camera image
only the center of the larger projected field. There the image is
\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{proj}}(184) = 184 \times \tfrac{65.6}{92} = 131.2\text{\,mm}, \qquad
  H_{\text{proj}}(184) = 184 \times \tfrac{41}{92} = 82\text{\,mm},
\end{equation}
so the projector over-fills the camera's $87.47\times54.67$\,mm footprint; the
camera sees its central $87.47/131.2 = 66.7\%$ in each axis. Compared with the
$122.7$\,mm fill:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Focus} is guaranteed at the rated distance --- no refocus
        assumption.
  \item \textbf{Resolution} is coarser: the projected pixel stays at its rated
        $100\,\mu$m (vs.\ $67\,\mu$m), and only $0.667^2 \approx 44\%$ of the
        DMD falls inside the camera view; the rest is wasted.
  \item \textbf{The reconstruction constant $W_{\text{proj}}$ changes.} The
        fringe carrier now spans the full $131.2$\,mm image, so
        $W_{\text{proj}} = 131.2$\,mm and the equivalent wavelength grows to
        $\lambda_{eq} = (131.2/n_p)/\tan\theta$ --- $1.5\times$ the fill-mode
        value, i.e.\ less height sensitivity per fringe. Raising the fringe
        count by $131.2/87.47 = 1.5\times$ restores the fill-mode fringe
        density across the measured region.
  \item The tilt $\theta \approx 38.7^\circ$ is \emph{unchanged}: the covered
        surface width $W_0/\cos\theta = 87.47$\,mm is a camera property; the
        projector merely over-covers it.
\end{itemize}
In code this mode is $D_{\text{PROJ}} = 184$, $W_{\text{PROJ}} = 131.2$, the
throw ratios unchanged, and $n_p$ scaled up by $1.5\times$; the $122.7$\,mm
fill remains the default.

\section{Telecentric footprint under an oblique view}

A telecentric lens's imaging cone is a bundle of parallel rays with
cross-section $W_0 \times H_0$, measured in the plane perpendicular to the
camera's optical axis. Tilting that axis by $\theta$ from the surface
normal is equivalent to a beam of width $W_0$ striking a plane at
incidence angle $\theta$: the illuminated footprint stretches by
$1/\cos\theta$ along the tilt direction, while the dimension
perpendicular to the tilt (here, height) is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  W(\theta) = \frac{W_0}{\cos\theta}, \qquad H(\theta) = H_0.
\end{equation}

\section{Solving for \texorpdfstring{$\theta$}{theta}}
\label{sec:solving-theta}

Require the camera's tilted width to cover the projector's image width at
the matching distance $D$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{W_0}{\cos\theta} = W_{\text{proj}}(D) = 1.6\, H_0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \cos\theta = \frac{W_0 / H_0}{1.6}.
\end{equation}
Notably, $W_0/H_0 = w_{\text{sensor}}/h_{\text{sensor}}$ --- the
magnification $M$ (and hence the absolute field-of-view scale) cancels
out. The required tilt depends only on the sensor's aspect ratio and the
projector's image aspect ratio:
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta = \frac{6.14/4.92}{1.6} = \frac{1.2480}{1.6} = 0.7800
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \theta \approx 38.7^\circ.
\end{equation}

\section{Caveats}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The projector's image-size-vs-distance law is a first-order
        (thin-lens, linear) approximation calibrated on the 184\,mm
        lens's datasheet point. Operating at $122.7$\,mm is $\sim$33\%
        inside its nominal distance; the brochure does not document the
        lens's focus range, so this modest refocus should be confirmed on
        the bench (far less of a reach than the unused $700$\,mm lens,
        which would need $153$\,mm --- $4.6\times$ below its nominal).
  \item The telecentric lens is assumed to fill the sensor format
        uniformly in both axes, i.e.\ FOV aspect ratio equals sensor
        aspect ratio.
  \item At $\theta \approx 38.7^\circ$, points across the tilted
        footprint sit at different distances along the camera's optical
        axis; a Scheimpflug-adjusted mount (or reliance on the lens's
        $\pm 63.6$\,mm depth of field at $f/10$) is needed to keep the
        whole footprint in focus.
\end{itemize}

\section{Projected fringe intensity}
\label{sec:fringe-intensity}

The remaining sections derive the phase-shifting reconstruction algorithm
used by \texttt{simulation/reconstruct.py}, kept consistent with the
geometric treatment in Samara, \emph{Fringe Projection System} (the two
reference PDFs under \texttt{docs/.local/}) --- in particular its
Eq.~2-27--2-29 (carrier phase / height-induced phase split, equivalent
wavelength) and Eq.~2-30--2-33 (the telecentric-arm height relation).
Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase} specializes that general result to
this rig's specific (asymmetric) geometry.

For a point at world position $(x,y,z)$ on the surface, the projector (a
perspective point source at normal incidence, Sections~1--6) casts a
normalized coordinate
\begin{equation}
  u(x,z) = \frac{x}{D-z}\cdot\frac{D}{W_{\text{proj}}(D)} + \frac12,
\end{equation}
identical to the perspective divide already used to size the footprint
(Section~5), now evaluated at the true, possibly-deformed surface height
$z$ rather than assuming $z=0$. The projected intensity, for a fringe
pattern of $n_p$ periods across the footprint width and a phase step
$\delta_n$, is
\begin{equation}
  I_n(x,y,z) = \frac12 + \frac12\sin\bigl(2\pi n_p\, u(x,z) + \delta_n\bigr),
  \qquad \delta_n = \frac{2\pi n}{N},\ n = 0,\dots,N-1,
  \label{eq:intensity}
\end{equation}
inside the illuminated rectangle and $0$ outside it. (The implementation
uses a sine reference; Section~\ref{sec:nstep-psa} accounts for the
resulting constant offset against Samara's cosine convention.)

\section{N-step phase-shifting algorithm}
\label{sec:nstep-psa}

Samara's Eq.~2-27 writes the fringe intensity in the canonical form
$I = A + B\cos(\phi+\psi)$ and defers the actual per-pixel phase extraction
to third-party software (Veeco's Vision, \S ``Software and Methodology'').
The extraction formula supplied here is the standard $N$-bucket
least-squares algorithm, derived to match that same cosine convention.

Write $\phi$ for the total phase at a pixel (carrier + height-induced
shift) and expand
\begin{equation}
  I_n = A + B\cos(\phi+\delta_n) = A + \bigl[B\cos\phi\bigr]\cos\delta_n - \bigl[B\sin\phi\bigr]\sin\delta_n .
\end{equation}
For $N \geq 3$ steps evenly spaced over one full cycle
($\delta_n = 2\pi n/N$), the discrete sums are orthogonal:
$\sum_n \cos\delta_n = \sum_n \sin\delta_n = \sum_n \sin\delta_n\cos\delta_n = 0$,
$\sum_n \cos^2\delta_n = \sum_n \sin^2\delta_n = N/2$. Projecting $I_n$ onto
$\cos\delta_n$ and $\sin\delta_n$ isolates
\begin{equation}
  B\cos\phi = \frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} I_n\cos\delta_n, \qquad
  B\sin\phi = -\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} I_n\sin\delta_n,
\end{equation}
so the wrapped phase follows from the ratio, independent of the unknowns
$A$ and $B$ (i.e.\ independent of the pixel's unknown background level and
fringe contrast):
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \phi = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(-\sum_n I_n \sin\delta_n,\ \sum_n I_n \cos\delta_n\right)\ }.
  \label{eq:nstep-psa}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the sign convention Husam's canonical DSP core uses
(cf.\ \texttt{RECONCILIATION.md} \S5.1) --- deliberately matched here so a
future merge doesn't re-open the sin/cos basis question.

Equation~\ref{eq:intensity} is a \emph{sine}, not cosine, reference: $I_n =
\frac12+\frac12\sin(\Phi+\delta_n) = \frac12+\frac12\cos(\Phi-\frac\pi2+\delta_n)$,
i.e.\ Eq.~\ref{eq:nstep-psa} applied to the simulator's frames recovers
$\phi = \Phi - \pi/2 \pmod{2\pi}$ rather than $\Phi$ itself. This constant
$-\pi/2$ offset is identical for every pixel and every capture (it comes
from the choice of reference waveform, not the geometry), so it cancels
exactly in the height-induced phase difference $\psi$ defined next.

\section{Height from phase, for this rig's geometry}
\label{sec:height-from-phase}

Samara's Eq.~2-30--2-33 gives the height-to-phase relation for a
\emph{symmetric or general telecentric two-arm} system: a bump of height
$h$ shifts the world-plane point a fixed camera ray images by
$U = h(\tan\theta_1+\tan\theta_2)$, giving
$h = \lambda_{eq}\psi/2\pi$ with $\lambda_{eq} = Mp/(\tan\theta_1+\tan\theta_2)$.
This rig is the asymmetric special case $\theta_1 = 0$ (projector at
normal incidence, Section~1): only the camera's tilt contributes,
\begin{equation}
  U = h\tan\theta, \qquad \lambda_{eq} = \frac{p_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta},
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{eff}}$ is the fringe pitch on the world/surface plane
(mm/cycle) rather than Samara's $Mp$ (grating period converted through the
camera-side magnification $M$) --- equivalent quantities, just expressed
directly in world units here since the reconstruction works in
world-plane mm throughout rather than converting through pixel space.

\paragraph{Derivation (camera side).} The telecentric camera collects
parallel rays along a fixed direction; a given pixel's ray hits the flat
reference plane ($z=0$) at world point $x_c$. If the surface instead sits
at height $h$ near that point, the same ray (unchanged direction) hits the
plane $z=h$ instead, at $x = x_c + h\tan\theta$ (elementary trigonometry:
raising the intersection plane by $h$ shortens the ray's remaining vertical
drop by $h$, shifting its horizontal travel by $h\tan\theta$). This is
purely a camera-side fact --- independent of whether the projector is
telecentric or a point source, unlike Samara's derivation which assumes
both arms share the same optics.

\paragraph{Projector side.} The intensity pattern is designed in $x$ (Eq.~
\ref{eq:intensity}) with known period $p_{\text{eff}} = W_{\text{proj}}(D)/n_p$
at the footprint center. To first order in $h/D$ (identical in spirit to
Samara's repeated linearizations, e.g.\ dropping the $x_1\tan\theta \ll a$
term in Eq.~2-46), the projector's own perspective divide is evaluated at
$z=0$ rather than $z=h$; the resulting error is $O(h/D) \approx 3/122.7 \approx
2.4\%$, small relative to $\lambda_{eq}$ below and neglected, exactly as
Samara neglects analogous second-order terms (e.g.\ Eq.~4-16).

\paragraph{Combining.} A fixed pixel's measured phase shifts by
$\psi = 2\pi\,x_{\text{shift}}/p_{\text{eff}} = 2\pi\,h\tan\theta/p_{\text{eff}}$,
so
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ h = \frac{\psi}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{p_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta} = \frac{\psi}{2\pi}\lambda_{eq}\ },
  \qquad \lambda_{eq} = \frac{p_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta}.
  \label{eq:height-from-phase}
\end{equation}
With $W_{\text{proj}}(D) = 87.47$\,mm, $n_p = 8$, $\theta \approx 38.7^\circ$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\text{eff}} = \frac{87.47}{8} = 10.934\text{\,mm/cycle}, \qquad
  \tan\theta = 0.8023, \qquad
  \lambda_{eq} = \frac{10.934}{0.8023} \approx 13.63\text{\,mm}.
\end{equation}

Per pixel, $\psi = \phi_{\text{measured}} - \phi_{\text{carrier}}$, where
$\phi_{\text{carrier}}$ is what Eq.~\ref{eq:nstep-psa} would measure at
that same pixel for a \emph{flat} ($h=0$) reference --- computable directly
from the rig's known geometry (Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase}'s
camera-ray map plus Eq.~\ref{eq:intensity} at $z=0$), not a separate
calibration capture, since the whole rig geometry here is already known
exactly rather than fit from measurements.

\section{Expected phase range}

For the validation surface (\texttt{simulation/surfaces.py}, amplitude
$3$\,mm), the largest expected phase shift is
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\max} = \frac{2\pi \times 3}{\lambda_{eq}} = \frac{2\pi\times3}{13.63}
  \approx 1.38\text{\,rad} \approx 79.2^\circ,
\end{equation}
comfortably inside $(-\pi,\pi]$. A single fringe frequency therefore
resolves this bump's phase unambiguously. Measuring \emph{roughness},
however, demands a much smaller $\lambda_{eq}$ (finer fringes) for vertical
resolution --- past the point where a single frequency wraps ---
motivating the multi-frequency ladder of
Section~\ref{sec:multifreq} and the roughness extraction of
Section~\ref{sec:roughness}.

{\text{carrier}}$'s slope) locally per pixel rather than once globally ---
worth doing if off-center accuracy matters, not done here since even the
worst case (\texttt{twin\_bump}) stays under $0.2$\,mm RMSE against
$2$--$3.5$\,mm features.

\section{Off-center bias: a follow-up, and a reverted fix}
\label{sec:off-center-followup}

Section~\ref{sec:validation-battery}'s explanation (constant $p_{\text{eff}}$
not accounting for the projector's off-axis perspective divide) predicts a
specific closed form: re-deriving $h$ from $\psi$ without dropping the
$x/D$ term in Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase}'s linearization gives

\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{eq}(x) = \frac{p_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta + x/D}
\end{equation}

in place of the constant $p_{\text{eff}}/\tan\theta$. This was implemented
and initially looked like an improvement: RMSE on \texttt{crater} dropped
from $0.160$ to $0.051$\,mm, \texttt{offset\_bump} from $0.186$ to
$0.093$\,mm, \texttt{twin\_bump} from $0.156$ to $0.058$\,mm.

Checking it against the actual per-pixel bias in the captured
\texttt{bump} data (not just the top-line RMSE) showed this was the wrong
conclusion. The predicted bias, $x h/(W_{\text{proj}}D) \cdot \lambda_{eq}$,
is $6$--$10\times$ smaller than the bias actually observed, and the wrong
shape: the true bias is antisymmetric in $x$ (binned mean bias at
$x=+32.75$\,mm and $x=-32.75$\,mm are $+0.057$ and $-0.057$\,mm --
essentially exact mirror images) but peaks around $|x|\approx24$\,mm,
whereas $xh(x)$ for this bump (a Gaussian, $\sigma=15$\,mm) peaks at
$|x|=\sigma=15$\,mm. A candidate explanation not yet derived or tested:
local magnification. The camera-ray map in
Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase} is exact for a single ray hitting a
tilted surface at one point, but two \emph{adjacent} rays hitting a
\emph{sloped} surface land at a spacing scaled by
$(1+\,dh/dx\cdot\tan\theta)$ relative to their flat-reference spacing --
a foreshortening effect distinct from (and evidently larger than) the
projector-side one this section set out to fix.

Applying only the (real, but apparently secondary) projector-side
correction made the two \emph{centered} surfaces worse
(\texttt{bump}: $0.050\to0.071$\,mm; \texttt{ridge}:
$0.046\to0.098$\,mm) while helping the off-center ones -- consistent with
a partial correction whose residual noise sensitivity (dividing by a
smaller $\tan\theta+x/D$ amplifies phase noise into height noise for
$x<0$) outweighs its benefit when the larger, unmodeled bias is still
present. The fix was reverted rather than shipped: Table~3's numbers are
the unmodified pipeline. The local-magnification hypothesis above is
where the next attempt should start.

\section{Multi-frequency temporal phase unwrapping}
\label{sec:multifreq}

The single-frequency reconstruction of Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase}
recovers height only modulo one equivalent wavelength: from
Eq.~\ref{eq:height-from-phase}, $h = (\psi/2\pi)\,\lambda_{eq}$ with
$\psi\in(-\pi,\pi]$, so heights outside $\pm\lambda_{eq}/2$ alias. Vertical
resolution and unambiguous range are therefore in direct tension: a small
$\lambda_{eq}$ (dense fringes) is what resolves a fine, tens-of-micron
roughness, but a large form ($h\gg\lambda_{eq}/2$) then wraps it beyond
recovery --- exactly the dynamic-range limit Samara's Chapter~3 poses for
roughness measurement.

The resolution is fixed by projecting a \emph{ladder} of frequencies,
coarse to fine, and unwrapping temporally. For rung $i$ with $n_{p,i}$
periods, Eq.~\ref{eq:height-from-phase} gives $\lambda_{eq,i} =
(W_{\text{proj}}/n_{p,i})/\tan\theta$, and the $N$-step PSA
(Eq.~\ref{eq:nstep-psa}) yields a wrapped surface phase $\psi_i\in(-\pi,\pi]$.
The coarsest rung is chosen unambiguous over the whole height range
($|h|<\lambda_{eq,0}/2$) and taken at face value, $h_0 =
(\psi_0/2\pi)\,\lambda_{eq,0}$. Each finer rung's integer fringe order is
then pinned by the running (coarser) estimate:
\begin{equation}
  k_i = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{2\pi\,h_{i-1}/\lambda_{eq,i} -
  \psi_i}{2\pi}\right), \qquad
  h_i = \left(\frac{\psi_i}{2\pi} + k_i\right)\lambda_{eq,i}.
  \label{eq:unwrap}
\end{equation}
The finest rung's $h_i$ is the result: its resolution, with the ambiguity
removed. Eq.~\ref{eq:unwrap} picks the correct order provided the guide is
within $\pm\lambda_{eq,i}/2$ of the truth, i.e.\ the consecutive ratio
$\lambda_{eq,i-1}/\lambda_{eq,i} = n_{p,i}/n_{p,i-1}$ is not so large that
the coarser rung's own noise exceeds half a fine fringe. The implemented
ladder (\texttt{geometry\_constants.N\_PERIODS\_LADDER}) is $n_p =
8\to24\to80$ (ratios $3,\,3.\overline{3}$), giving $\lambda_{eq} =
13.6\to4.5\to1.4$\,mm: a $\pm6.8$\,mm unambiguous range with a
$1.4$\,mm final wavelength, so $\sim\!10\times$ the vertical resolution of
the coarse rung alone. \texttt{reconstruct.unwrap\_multifreq} implements
Eq.~\ref{eq:unwrap} for an arbitrary-length ladder; a higher-magnification
lens later simply supplies a denser one. In a clean forward-model test a
$3$\,mm bump --- which the $n_p=80$ rung alone reconstructs at $1.2$\,mm
RMSE (badly aliased) --- unwraps to $0.4$\,\textmu m RMSE ($R^2=1.0$), and
holds every fringe order under $3$\,DN of injected noise ($2.9$\,\textmu m).

\section{Roughness: form removal and areal parameters}
\label{sec:roughness}

The unwrapped height map $h(x,y)$ carries a large, smooth \emph{form}
(waviness) with a small, high-frequency \emph{roughness} on top. The two
are separated by spatial wavelength with an ISO~16610-61 Gaussian
regression filter: the form is $h$ convolved with a Gaussian of cutoff
wavelength $\lambda_c$,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_g = \lambda_c\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{2\pi^2}} \approx 0.1874\,\lambda_c,
\end{equation}
and the roughness is the residual $r = h - (h * g_{\sigma_g})$. The
convolution is mask-aware (a normalized convolution over the valid
region, $\text{form} = (h\,m)*g \,/\, m*g$ with $m$ the valid mask) so
unlit pixels at the field boundary do not drag a false slope into the
form. Everything coarser than $\lambda_c$ is form, everything finer
roughness; $\lambda_c$ must sit between the two bands. On the residual,
over the valid region and relative to its mean plane, the ISO~25178 areal
parameters follow directly:
\begin{equation}
  S_a = \frac1A\!\int|r|\,dA, \quad
  S_q = \sqrt{\frac1A\!\int r^2\,dA}, \quad
  S_z = \max r + |\!\min r|,
\end{equation}
with $S_p,S_v,S_{sk},S_{ku}$ likewise (\texttt{roughness.py}).

\emph{Noise floor.} Per-pixel height noise $\sigma_h$
(Section~\ref{sec:height-from-phase}'s $\lambda_{eq}$ divided into the
phase noise; estimated on the finest rung by \texttt{noise\_estimate.py})
is broadband and passes the high-pass, so it adds in quadrature to the RMS
roughness:
\begin{equation}
  S_q^{\text{meas}\,2} = S_q^{\text{true}\,2} + \sigma_h^2
  \ \Rightarrow\
  S_q^{\text{true}} = \sqrt{\max(S_q^{\text{meas}\,2} - \sigma_h^2,\,0)},
  \label{eq:sq-denoise}
\end{equation}
and the roughness SNR is $S_q/\sigma_h$. Against a synthetic specimen
(\texttt{surfaces.rough}: a $1.5$\,mm dome plus a band-limited sinusoid
texture, true $S_q=19.9$\,\textmu m), the ladder recovers $S_a/S_q$ to
$\pm0.1$\,\textmu m clean, and Eq.~\ref{eq:sq-denoise} returns the true
$S_q$ ($19.9$\,\textmu m) at $0$, $2$ and $5$\,DN of noise (raw $S_q$
inflating to $20.5$\,\textmu m at $5$\,DN before correction). The point of
the ladder is stark here: the coarse rung alone, with its $\sim\!10\times$
larger noise floor ($\approx19$\,\textmu m at $2$\,DN), reads
$S_q\approx27.6$\,\textmu m --- the roughness is entirely below its floor,
whereas the ladder brings the floor to $1.9$\,\textmu m (SNR $\approx10$).

\section{Resolution and roughness budget}
\label{sec:roughness-budget}

What roughness is measurable is set by a vertical noise floor, a lateral
limit, and a systematic-error term --- and tested against a real Blender
camera render (Section~\ref{sec:roughness-camera}), the binding one is not
what a fringe-pitch argument predicts. Vertically the random floor is
small: $\sigma_h\approx1.9$\,\textmu m on the forward model at $n_p=80$,
and on Blender the white (spatial) noise is only $\approx0.5$\,\textmu m.
The temporal noise estimate is far larger there ($\approx11$ vs
$0.5$\,DN), but that gap is a smooth systematic render artifact, not random
noise; form-removal deletes most of it, so it is reported as a
\emph{systematic} flag rather than folded into the floor
(\texttt{roughness.py}, \texttt{SYSTEMATIC\_RATIO\_THRESH}). So this rig
targets $S_a/S_q$ in the tens-of-microns-and-up band --- mid-spatial
waviness, not sub-micron finish. A naive fringe-pitch argument would put
the \emph{lateral} limit at $\sim\!2$--$3\times$ the finest projected
period ($W_{\text{proj}}/n_p=87.5/80\approx1.1$\,mm, i.e.\
$\gtrsim2.5$\,mm); Section~\ref{sec:roughness-camera} shows that is wrong
--- the real limit is geometric registration, and a denser rung does not
move it. Finer roughness therefore needs a geometry calibration first, and
a higher-magnification lens over a smaller field (crop-mode spirit,
Section~\ref{sec:crop}) --- which the code
(\texttt{N\_PERIODS\_LADDER}, \texttt{geometry\_constants.py}) makes a
configuration change, not a rewrite.

\section{Roughness under a real camera}
\label{sec:roughness-camera}

The forward-model validation (Section~\ref{sec:roughness}) cannot test the
\emph{camera}: it synthesizes intensities from the reconstruction's own
equations, recovering roughness tautologically. To test capture, the
\texttt{rough} specimen's coarse$\to$fine ladder ($n_p=8,24,80$, eight
frames each) was rendered through Blender's ray-traced telecentric camera
--- the surface carried as a $10^6$-vertex displaced mesh, since the
default $\sim\!1.7$\,mm grid is far too coarse for sub-mm texture
(\texttt{rig.add\_surface}'s \texttt{subdivisions}) --- and
\texttt{roughness.py} run on the rendered frames.

The aggregate parameters survive: $S_a=16.7$ (true $16.8$), $S_q=21.3$
(true $20.9$)\,\textmu m, within $0.4$\,\textmu m. But the per-pixel
roughness \emph{map} correlates with truth at only $r=0.73$ ($R^2=0.53$)
--- until one allows a rigid lateral shift: a single $+8$\,px
($\approx0.55$\,mm) offset along $x$ (the tilt/throw axis) raises it to
$r=0.92$. The recovered texture is faithful in amplitude and shape; it is
simply \emph{mis-registered} by $\sim\!0.5$\,mm, by the same
nominal-geometry error
(Sections~\ref{sec:validation-battery}--\ref{sec:off-center-followup}) that
prints a $134$\,\textmu m form RMSE. Consistent with a registration --- not
a resolution --- limit, adding a denser $n_p=160$ rung
($\lambda_{eq}\approx0.68$\,mm, half the fringe period) changed the
recovered roughness by \emph{nothing} (identical band correlations,
$0.82/0.75/0.67/0.63$ at $6/4/3/2.5$\,mm), and was reverted. The fix is a
geometry calibration, not more fringes or samples --- the same calibration
the height reconstruction already wants, now quantified for roughness:
without it the texture lands $\sim\!0.5$\,mm off; with the offset removed,
$r=0.92$.
